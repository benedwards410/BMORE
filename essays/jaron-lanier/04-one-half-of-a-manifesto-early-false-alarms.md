# The Alarm That Went Off Early

### One-Half of a Manifesto (2000), and why early false alarms are worth the embarrassment

## The moment

In the autumn of 2000 Jaron Lanier published a long essay on Edge.org, John Brockman's forum for scientists and technologists, under the odd title "One-Half of a Manifesto." Wired reprinted it that December. The timing matters. The dot-com bubble was deflating but had not yet burst. Ray Kurzweil's The Age of Spiritual Machines (1999) sat on every engineer's shelf. Bill Joy, co-founder of Sun Microsystems, had published "Why the Future Doesn't Need Us" in Wired that spring, warning that robotics, genetics, and nanotechnology could make humans obsolete. The conversation in Silicon Valley was not whether machines would surpass people, but when.

Lanier's target was not the machines. It was the belief.

## The belief

He gave it a name, "cybernetic totalism," and he listed its tenets so they could be examined one at a time. Six of them:

1. Cybernetic patterns of information are the ultimate and best way to understand reality.
2. People are no more than cybernetic patterns.
3. Subjective experience either does not exist or is unimportant, a side effect.
4. What Darwin described in biology is the single best description of all creativity and culture.
5. The qualitative as well as the quantitative aspects of information systems will be accelerated by Moore's Law.
6. Biology and physics will merge with computer science, life itself will become as fluid as software, and all of this will happen soon. Computers will overwhelm every other process, people included, at some moment of criticality, perhaps around the year 2020. To be human after that will be impossible or unrecognizable.

He then did what manifestos rarely do: he argued against each. Software, he pointed out, does not obey Moore's Law. Hardware doubles; software gets bigger, more brittle, and harder to change. A small flaw anywhere breaks the whole. Any intelligence that depends on such systems inherits their brittleness.

His sharpest point was about the Turing test. The test can be passed in two directions. Either the machine gets smart enough to seem human, or humans lower their standards until the machine seems smart. He worried that a culture convinced of tenet number two would take the second path without noticing. People would make themselves stupid in order to make their computers look intelligent.

The essay was half a manifesto because it offered the critique and not the alternative. Lanier said so in the title. He was describing what was wrong, not prescribing what to do, and he was honest enough to label the missing half.

## The false alarm

Measured as a prediction, the essay gets an easy verdict. The year 2020 arrived and nothing like tenet number six occurred. Humans were still human, and quite busy with a pandemic. The AI that existed in 2020 recommended videos, filled in search boxes, and, inside one research lab, had just begun to write passable paragraphs. Nobody called it a merger of biology and software.

Meanwhile the specific failure the essay predicted, software collapsing under its own weight, did not arrive either. Software got better at scale. Cloud computing, mobile phones, and open-source tooling delivered systems far larger and more reliable than anything in 2000. The essay's engineering pessimism looked like the complaint of a man whose own hardware company had gone bankrupt.

By the standards of a weather forecast, then, Lanier cried wolf. Edge published a chorus of rebuttals, from Daniel Dennett, Rodney Brooks, Kevin Kelly, and Bruce Sterling among others, and many of them said roughly that: the alarm was premature, the tenets were straw men, the fears were old. Some of the responses were sharper than the essay. None of them aged better.

Here is the argument of this essay: that verdict is wrong, not because the prediction came true, but because prediction is the wrong way to grade an alarm.

## What an alarm is for

A smoke detector that goes off when you burn toast has not malfunctioned. It has detected smoke. Whether a fire followed is not the measure of its value. The measure is whether a fire could have followed and whether you had time to check.

Early warnings in technology are smoke detectors. They are triggered by a pattern, not by a catastrophe, and their job is to make people look up. The ten months before Lanier's essay had supplied the best example in living memory. On the first of January 2000, the Y2K bug produced almost no failures, after the world had spent hundreds of billions of dollars fixing it. The result was immediately read as proof that the alarm had been overblown. The opposite was true. The alarm had worked so well that it erased the evidence of its own necessity.

This is the central trap of early warnings. If they succeed, they look unnecessary. If they are ignored and the disaster comes, they are vindicated at the worst possible price. The only way to be proven right in public is for everyone to have failed to listen.

## Four reasons early false alarms matter

**1. They set the vocabulary.** Before "One-Half of a Manifesto," the view that a person is a pattern of information was ambient, unnamed, and therefore unarguable. Lanier named it. For a quarter century, every debate about the Singularity, machine consciousness, digital immortality, and what it would mean to "upload" a mind has been conducted on the terrain he mapped. You cannot argue with a mood. You can argue with a tenet.

**2. They are cheap.** The cost of a false alarm is some embarrassment and some wasted attention. The cost of a late true alarm is the disaster plus the alarm. Any sane society should tolerate many false positives to catch one true negative, and should treat the person willing to be embarrassed early as doing an unpaid service. Y2K cost a fortune and prevented a fortune. Nobody can prove which was larger, and that is the point.

**3. They train the response.** Institutions do not improvise well. Ethics boards, review processes, and the habit of asking "what does this do to people" exist in 2026 because the questions were raised long before the systems that needed them. The rehearsals looked silly. Rehearsals do.

**4. "False" is usually just "early."** Lanier's deadline of 2020 passed. Then, in 2022, systems appeared that could write fluent prose about anything. A Google engineer announced that one of them was sentient and was fired. Within months, millions of people were treating chat interfaces as oracles, and the whole culture was arguing about whether the machines understood, felt, or deserved rights. This was not tenet number six. It was tenet number two, arriving by the second path through the Turing test, exactly as described: not machines rising to meet us, but a public lowering the bar to meet machines. The alarm was not false. The clock was slow.

## The boy who cried wolf, reread

The fable is always told as a warning against false alarms. Read it again. The wolf comes. The village's error was not that it believed the boy the first two times. It was that it stopped listening on the third. A community that punishes early alarms does not get fewer wolves. It gets quieter boys.

Lanier paid the usual price. For most of the 2000s his essay was cited as the work of a pioneer who had turned gloomy. It became the piece quoted by people who wanted to say that fears about AI were old and tired. The irony is that the people quoting it were, by doing so, enacting tenet number two: treating the question of what a person is as settled, and settled in the machine's favor.

## What the half left out

The missing half, the constructive program, took Lanier the rest of his career to write, and it is not finished. His later work argues for keeping people visible inside the systems built from them. Whether that program succeeds is a separate question. What the 2000 essay established was only this: before you let a belief system decide what you are, read its tenets aloud and check whether you agree.

## The verdict from 2026

Grade "One-Half of a Manifesto" as a forecast and it fails on the date. Grade it as an alarm and it did exactly what an alarm should do. It went off before the fire, it named the smoke, and it gave twenty-two years of warning to a culture that mostly used the time to laugh at it.

The right response to an early false alarm is not to rip out the detector. It is to look up, check the kitchen, and thank whoever installed it. Lanier installed this one in 2000. The kitchen is now full of smoke. Whether it becomes a fire is, as he said at the time, still up to the people, provided they remember that they are not patterns.
