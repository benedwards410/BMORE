# Who Owns the Future? (2013) on Trial

*Format: the docket, the prosecution, the defense, the judgment. About 300, 300, 300, and 100 words.*

## The docket

Jaron Lanier's Who Owns the Future? appeared in 2013, between the financial crisis and the rise of large AI models, and argued that the two were connected by a single mechanism.

The mechanism: digital networks make human contribution invisible and therefore free. His opening example is machine translation. Automatic translators do not understand languages. They are built from millions of pages translated by human beings, scraped and recombined. The humans are not paid for the reuse, and the better the machine gets, the fewer of them can earn a living. Multiply that across music, journalism, photography, retail, logistics, and driving, and the result is an economy in which the top network operators grow while the paid middle shrinks.

Lanier calls the protections that once held the middle class in place "levees": unions, professional licenses, copyright, tenure, pensions. He argues that the digital economy is washing them away, and that the usual answers, either cheering the flood or rebuilding the old walls, both fail.

His alternative is a "humanistic information economy." It rests on an old idea from Ted Nelson, the inventor of hypertext: two-way links. If every piece of information on the network pointed back to its origin, provenance would be permanent, and a payment, however small, could flow to the originator whenever the data was used. Over a lifetime these nanopayments would add up. People would be paid for the data they generate, including the data that trains the machines that replace them. Automation could proceed without wiping out the people it learned from.

A year after publication, Lanier received the Peace Prize of the German Book Trade, largely for the argument this book makes. It was read widely in Silicon Valley, which is to say it was praised, argued with, and not implemented. The charge today is whether it was right.

## The prosecution

The prosecution's case is that the book is a beautiful diagnosis attached to a prescription no pharmacy can fill.

**Count one: the architecture does not exist.** Two-way links were proposed in the 1960s and never built. Nelson's Project Xanadu spent decades failing to ship. The web won precisely because it allowed one-way links with no central ledger. Retrofitting provenance onto every byte of a network designed to discard it is not a policy. It is a rebuild of the internet, and Lanier offers no path from here to there. Every byte copied without a backlink is a debt the system can never collect.

**Count two: the arithmetic does not work.** Most individual data is worth a fraction of a cent. A lifetime of nanopayments, spread across billions of people, delivers pocket change to each while requiring an accounting system larger than the economy it tracks. The administrative cost swallows the benefit.

**Count three: it lets power off the hook.** By blaming "architecture," the book treats monopoly as weather. But the companies it describes made choices: to scrape without consent, to crush competitors, to lobby against regulation. The remedies for that are old and boring: antitrust, labor law, privacy law. Nanopayments are a distraction that keeps people inside the same platforms, now as paid serfs rather than unpaid ones.

**Count four: it fires on its own side.** The book lumps Wikipedia and open-source software in with the data-harvesting giants, as if a volunteer commons and a surveillance business were the same threat. That alienated the people most likely to build an alternative.

**Count five: it romanticizes the levees.** The mid-century middle class rested on protections that were also exclusions. Lanier's nostalgia for them is selective.

The prosecution rests: right about the symptom, wrong about the cure, and silent on who would ever be made to pay for it.

## The defense

The defense calls one witness: the year 2026.

**The diagnosis was literal, not metaphorical.** The translation example, which read in 2013 as a parable, is now the exact architecture of the most valuable products on earth. Large language models are built by scraping the written output of humanity, uncredited and unpaid, and then selling access to the recombination. Every objection about "most data being worth nothing" has been answered by companies spending billions on compute to extract value from that supposedly worthless data.

**The market already pays for data, just not to people.** Since 2023, AI companies have signed licensing deals with news publishers, stock-image libraries, and Reddit, whose users wrote the content being licensed. Lanier's "utopian" claim that data has a price was conceded the moment the aggregators started writing checks. The only part still unbuilt is the part where the money reaches the originator. That is not a refutation of the book. It is the book's thesis.

**Provenance is now policy.** Content-credential standards attach origin metadata to images and video. European AI rules require makers of general-purpose models to publish a summary of their training data. Two-way links are being rebuilt piecemeal, by regulators and standards bodies, because the problem Lanier named became impossible to ignore.

**"Data as labor" became a field.** Economists, including Lanier's own collaborators, turned the book's intuition into formal models of data markets and collective bargaining over data. A thought experiment that spawns a research program is not a failure.

**The risk transfer was forecast.** The book's account of networks that keep the gains and push the losses outward is a precise description of the gig economy that was then only beginning.

The defense rests: the prosecution has convicted the author of not finishing the job, which he admitted in the book itself.

## Judgment

**On the diagnosis: acquitted.** The book described, a decade early, the mechanism by which machine systems are built from unpaid human work. That mechanism now runs the largest industry on earth.

**On the remedy: guilty of incompleteness, not of error.** Nanopayments remain unbuilt. But the burden has shifted. In 2013 the question was whether anyone would ever pay for data. In 2026 the question is only who gets the money.

**Sentence:** the book is to be read again, this time as a description of the present, with the final chapters read as a sketch rather than a plan.
