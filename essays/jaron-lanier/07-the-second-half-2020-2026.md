# The Second Half: Lockdown to the Fork (2020 to 2026)

### Continues "One-Half of a Manifesto (2000): Why Early False Alarms Matter." Fills the placeholders in the October 23, 2026 draft, from "Then came Covid" to the fork in the road.

**The thread.** Lanier's 2000 alarm said the danger was not machines getting smart but people bending themselves to fit software. In March 2020 the bending stopped being a metaphor. For most of two years, school, work, food, friendship, and family logistics ran through a screen, and the screen kept the records. Here is what the data say happened, section by section, and where it leaves us.

## Children and schools

Most of the country's roughly 50 million public school students were sent home in March 2020, and a large share stayed remote or hybrid into 2021. The instrument that measures the result is the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP).

| NAEP marker | Result |
|---|---|
| 2022, grades 4 and 8 | Largest math drops in the test's history; reading down in both grades |
| 2024, grades 4 and 8 | Reading fell again, 5 points below 2019 in both grades; about 40% of 4th graders below Basic in reading, the most since 2002; 4th grade math up 2 points, 8th grade flat |
| 2024, grade 12 (released Sept 2025) | Lowest reading and math scores on record; 45% below Basic in math; 22% proficient; the top tenth held steady while everyone else fell |

The last row is the Lanier row. The students who were 8th graders in lockdown reached 12th grade with the widest gap between the top and the rest the test has recorded. The screen did not flatten. It sorted.

## Adults and work

Paid workdays done from home went from about 5 percent before 2020 to 61 percent in April and May 2020, then settled near a quarter, where they have stayed through 2025. Zoom went from 10 million daily meeting participants in December 2019 to more than 300 million in April 2020. Parents of young children work from home seven points more than everyone else, and in every demographic group people want more remote days than they get.

The surprise is productivity. After a weak decade, U.S. output per hour rose sharply in 2025, with full-year projections near 2.7 percent. Whether AI caused it is unsettled. Where the gain went is not. See the wealth row below.

## Meal services

The pandemic turned food into a data stream twice. Delivery apps roughly tripled their order volume in 2020 and kept the habit afterward. Meanwhile the state fed children directly: federal waivers made school meals free for every student from spring 2020 until June 30, 2022, when Congress let them lapse and about 10 million children went back to means-tested applications. Emergency SNAP allotments ended in early 2023. The free-meal years are the clearest natural experiment the period produced. When the paperwork disappeared, participation rose. When it came back, participation fell.

## Parent meetings, kids' sports, 4-H

Every parent-teacher conference, team sign-up, scout meeting, and 4-H project moved to video for a year or more. In September 2020 the Aspen Institute found that nearly 3 in 10 children who had played sports were no longer interested, practice time was down 54 percent, and game time down 59 percent. Regular team participation for ages 6 to 17 stayed about 6 percent below 2019 through 2022. By 2025 it had recovered to the highest level since 2019. The children came back. The logistics did not: sign-ups, schedules, payments, and photos stayed on platforms, which is to say they stayed in someone's database.

## Bad actors, innocent victims

| Harm | Scale |
|---|---|
| Pandemic unemployment insurance fraud | $100 billion to $135 billion of about $900 billion paid (GAO estimate) |
| PPP and EIDL loan fraud | $200 billion potentially fraudulent (SBA inspector general); the agency itself says $36 billion |
| Reports of online child sexual exploitation (NCMEC CyberTipline) | 16.9 million (2019), 21.7 million (2020), 29.3 million (2021), 36.2 million (2023) |
| Consumer fraud losses reported to the FTC | $8.8 billion (2022), $10 billion (2023), $12.5 billion (2024) |

The victims were the people with the least slack: children online all day, elderly people reached by phone and text, and honest claimants whose benefits were delayed by fraud filters. The systems that made relief fast made theft fast. That is the Siren Server shape again: the platform collects, and the risk lands on whoever is at the other end.

## The backlash, and the data since

The backlash came in three waves. Parents turned on school closures in 2021 and 2022. The Surgeon General issued a formal advisory on social media and youth mental health in May 2023. Then came the phone bans: by mid-2025 at least 22 states and the District of Columbia had passed laws or policies restricting phones in class, with more following.

| Measure | Then | Latest |
|---|---|---|
| Teen girls reporting persistent sadness (CDC) | 57% in 2021 | 53% in 2023 |
| Prices (CPI) | about 1% a year before 2020 | up more than a fifth since early 2020, closer to a quarter by 2026; 8% in 2022 alone; the poorest fifth saw the most |
| Real median household income | fell 2020 to 2022 | record $87,460 in 2025, up 2.6% on 2024 |
| Wealth share of the top 1% (Federal Reserve) | about 31% in 2019 | 32.5% in mid-2026; the bottom half holds 2.3% |
| Reading, grade 12 | pre-pandemic norms | record low, 2024 |

Read the table as a whole. Incomes recovered. Prices did not come back down. Wealth concentrated further. The children's scores did not recover. Capability went up and the human baseline went down, which is the exact pattern the half-manifesto described: software gets better, people get bent.

## The rise of AI, 2024 to 2026

ChatGPT went from nothing in November 2022 to 400 million weekly users in February 2025 and 900 million in February 2026, with reports of a billion later in the year. The four largest American technology companies spent about $410 billion on capital expenditure in 2025 and guided to roughly $725 billion to $760 billion for 2026, most of it AI infrastructure. The compute used to train frontier models has doubled about every five months.

One item cuts the other way. In September 2025 Anthropic agreed to pay authors $1.5 billion to settle claims over books used in training, about $3,000 per book. It is the first time a large AI company paid individual contributors at scale for training data. Lanier's nanopayment, three thousand dollars at a time, arrived through a courtroom rather than an architecture.

## The threat of centralization

The fork is between two ways of getting intelligence.

**Rented.** A handful of companies, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, xAI, and now Meta, run closed frontier models behind an API. You send your data in. The model and the logs stay with them. Meta, which carried the open-weights banner with Llama, launched its first closed frontier model in April 2026 while promising smaller open releases.

**Owned.** The Hugging Face model: weights you can download, run, and modify. In 2026 the strongest open-weight models come mostly from Chinese labs, DeepSeek, Alibaba's Qwen, Moonshot's Kimi, and Z.ai's GLM, with OpenAI's gpt-oss and Google's Gemma from the American side. More than two million models sit on Hugging Face. Open models trail the closed frontier by months, not years.

Two cautions. Open weights are not open source: the training data and code stay private, so the public gets the artifact, not the recipe. And open weights decentralize use, not production. Only a few labs can afford a frontier training run, so who shapes the base model is still a question about a few companies and two governments.

## The private, local model

Can each citizen run a model that is private, local, and under their own control? For inference, yes, today. A 20 to 30 billion parameter open model runs on a good laptop. Apple ships a small on-device model with its phones. Ollama and LM Studio turn any Mac into a server. For training from scratch, no, and not soon: a frontier run costs hundreds of millions of dollars and the bar doubles every five months. What a citizen can do is personalize: fine-tune a small open model on their own documents, or give it retrieval over their own files, on hardware they own.

Here the question needs a correction. Lanier's data dignity is not "everyone trains their own model." It is provenance and payment inside shared models: trace which human contributions a model drew on, and pay them, through intermediaries he called mediators of individual data. A private local model solves privacy and control. It does not pay the people whose work built its base, and it does not stop a company from building a Siren Server on the training side. The two ideas are complements, not substitutes.

So the pathway at the fork is a stack, not a choice:

1. An open-weight base model as a commons.
2. A local, personal layer the citizen owns and runs.
3. Provenance and payment flowing upstream to the people in the base.
4. Limits on agents acting on your behalf without you, which is the step Lanier has pushed hardest in 2026.

## Is there a fork, and is the growth exponential?

There is a fork, and it is cheap to take now and expensive later. Both paths are open in 2026 because open models are close to the frontier and local hardware is good enough. Every year of lock-in, through API habits, enterprise contracts, and regulation written for the closed model, raises the price of the owned path. That is the same logic the 2000 essay used for software: warn before the architecture sets.

On exponential growth, one reading note: I take "modern civil aviation" in the brief as "modern civilization." Civilization's exponentials are real but narrow. Compute is up 4.7 times a year. AI users went from zero to nearly a billion in three years. Capital spending nearly doubled in a year. The human measures are not exponential: productivity up 2 to 3 percent, incomes up a couple of percent, reading scores down. For the literal reading, air travel hit a record in 2025 on 5.3 percent growth, which the industry calls a return to its historical pattern. Not exponential, and not new.

Hurtful or beneficial depends on which number you stand on. The exponentials belong to the machine side. The benefits have to be routed to the human side on purpose, which is the whole argument of data dignity.

## Describe the situation: one word

**Disappointing.** Not dystopian: the fork is open, the open models are good, a court made a company pay authors, and children's sports came back. Not exciting yet: the measured human baseline, reading, mental health, wealth share, got worse while the machines got better, and that gap is what Lanier's alarm was about. Disappointing is the honest word for a period in which capability outran distribution. Runner-up: exciting, because nothing in the fork is locked yet.

## The next debate dilemmas

1. **Open weights versus safety.** Anyone can download a frontier-class model, or five companies hold them behind APIs. Misuse on one side, concentration on the other. Pick.
2. **Agents.** Should an AI act for you or only with you? If it acts for you and is wrong, who is liable: you or the company that trained it?
3. **Who gets paid.** Reddit got paid. Authors got paid. The users whose posts trained the models got nothing. Is data dignity a right or a product?
4. **The school screen.** Ban the phone, mandate the AI tutor? Can a state restrict one screen while funding another?
5. **Control versus capability.** Will people accept a model a year behind the frontier in exchange for owning their data? If not, the owned path dies for lack of demand, not lack of code.
6. **What to count.** Benchmarks are exponential and report cards are falling. Which one is progress?
7. **Who keeps the productivity.** If AI delivers a 2.7 percent year and the top 1 percent already holds a third of the wealth, does the gain change that share or confirm it?

## Dilemmas 1, 2, 3 and 6, expanded

### 1. Open weights versus safety

**The dilemma.** A frontier-class model can be published as downloadable weights that anyone can run, copy, and modify, or it can be kept behind an API run by one of about five companies. Each choice removes a different kind of risk and creates a different kind of power.

**The case for open weights.** Price and access: DeepSeek's open release in January 2025 showed that a model months behind the frontier could be run at a fraction of the cost, and the gap has stayed at months, not years. Sovereignty: governments, hospitals, and companies can run intelligence they do not have to rent, on hardware they control, under laws they wrote. Audit: researchers can inspect what a closed vendor only describes. Resilience: nobody's terms of service can switch the model off. Even the closed labs conceded the point in part. OpenAI shipped its gpt-oss models under a permissive license in August 2025, and the July 2025 U.S. AI Action Plan told agencies to encourage open-weight models.

**The case for closed.** Irreversibility: weights, once published, cannot be recalled, and the safety training on them can be stripped with a small, cheap fine-tune. Uplift: the worry is not chatbots but help with biological and cyber attacks, where a single misuse can outweigh a million ordinary uses. Governance: rules written in 2026 cannot be applied to a file copied in 2025. And the open frontier in 2026 is mostly Chinese, DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, GLM, so "open" also means depending on another government's release decisions. Meta, the American standard-bearer for open weights, moved its frontier work closed in April 2026.

**What both sides miss.** Open weights decentralize use, not production. A frontier training run costs hundreds of millions of dollars, so the base model is still shaped by a few labs and two governments whichever way the weights go. Lanier's position cuts across the debate: the danger he names is people treating any model, open or closed, as an oracle, and letting agents act for them. He has argued against software agents since 1995. Open weights with no provenance are still a Siren Server for whoever hosts them. Closed weights with provenance and payment would at least keep the people inside the model visible.

**The motion to debate.** "This house believes the greater risk is concentration, not misuse." The side arguing misuse must explain how it will govern files it cannot recall. The side arguing concentration must explain why a Chinese open model is less of a dependency than an American closed one.

### 3. Who gets paid

**The ledger, as of 2026.** Platforms got paid: Reddit's licensing deal with Google was reported at about $60 million a year in 2024, and News Corp's with OpenAI at about $250 million over five years. Rights-holders with lawyers got paid: Anthropic agreed in September 2025 to pay authors $1.5 billion, about $3,000 a book. Users got nothing. The people who wrote the Reddit posts, the forum answers, the reviews, and the code comments that trained the models received no check, no credit, and no notice.

**The catch in the Anthropic money.** The June 2025 ruling in that case found that training on lawfully bought books was fair use. What was not fair use was downloading pirated copies. The $1.5 billion paid for how the books were obtained, not for what they contributed. So far, American law pays for the acquisition of data, not for the value it creates. That is the opposite of data dignity, which pays for value.

**Three ways the money can flow.**

| Route | Who gets paid | What it needs | Who it misses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Licensing | The platform that holds the data | A contract | Everyone who made the data |
| Litigation | Rights-holders who can sue | A statute and a class action | Everyone without a registered copyright |
| Data dignity | The individual contributor, continuously | Provenance inside the model, plus intermediaries to bargain | Nobody, if the tracing works |

**The one-route version.** A reader's position, sharper than the table: there should be one route, data dignity, resting on data ownership. The person owns the data. A collector may hold and use it only while it can show responsible collection and management, meaning consent at the point of collection, security in storage, and no breaches. Breach the condition and the license ends, or the collector pays.

What this adds. It turns three routes into one by making ownership the default and licensing the exception. It also supplies the enforcement the dignity route lacked: not a new payment rail, but liability. The precedents exist. Illinois's biometric privacy law sets statutory damages per violation without proof of harm, which produced a $650 million settlement from Facebook in 2021. Equifax paid up to $700 million for its 2017 breach. Breach notification is law in all fifty states. What does not exist, as of this writing, is a federal rule that the data is yours in the first place. Europe gives people rights over data without calling it ownership. The United States gives them notification.

The 2024 to 2026 record argues for the stricter version. The Change Healthcare breach exposed about 190 million people's records in 2024. Breaches did not slow as AI spending rose; the data got more valuable and the holders got bigger. Under the one-route rule, a model trained on data without the owner's license is not fair use with a piracy footnote. It is a breach, compensable per record, which is roughly what the Anthropic settlement looked like from the outside: a price per work.

The catch, and the fix. Compensation for breach pays for harm. Data dignity pays for value. A collector that is careful, consented, and never breached would, under the one-route rule, owe nothing while building a model worth billions from the data. That is the Siren Server with good security. So the one route needs both clauses: the license to collect is conditioned on responsible management (the reader's clause), and the license to use is conditioned on payment for the value created (Lanier's clause). Ownership is the ground. Dignity is the rent.

**Right or product.** If payment is a product, it is opt-in, market-priced, and in practice set to zero by terms of service, which is the current state. If it is a right, it is non-waivable, like a minimum wage, and the per-person triviality is solved the way wages solved it: collectively. Lanier and Weyl's 2018 blueprint proposed exactly that, mediators of individual data that bargain for groups of contributors the way unions bargain for workers. Reddit already behaves like a mediator for its users. It just keeps the money.

**The objections, and the answers.** Attribution is impossible at trillion-token scale: it is hard, and it is being worked on, and the European Union has required training-data summaries from general-purpose model makers since August 2025, which is the first step. Micropayments are trivial: per person, yes, which is why the mediator exists. It would entrench the platforms as paymasters: only if the mediators are the platforms, which is the design choice to fight over. Data is not labor: a fair philosophical point that does not change where the money is going.

**Why it is urgent now.** The old payment was traffic. People wrote for free because readers came, and readers brought ads, donations, and reputation. In 2025 human visits to Wikipedia fell by roughly eight percent as AI answers replaced clicks. When the model answers instead of the page, the traffic payment stops, and nothing replaces it. The fork here is not whether contributors get paid. It is whether the stop in payment is noticed before the contributors stop contributing.

**The motion to debate.** "This house would make payment for training data a non-waivable right of the individual contributor." The opposition's strongest line: users were paid in free services. The proposition's answer: so were the sailors who heard the Sirens.

### 2. Agents: act for only you

**The thesis.** An agent should act for only you, the way a lawyer or a broker owes loyalty to one client. Then let the free-market principle do the rest: each person's agent pursues that person's interest, and the system of many agents corrects itself through price, competition, and exit, the way markets of people do.

**Why the thesis is stronger than it sounds.** It is the fiduciary rule, and fiduciary rules are the oldest fix for the problem agents create. A doctor, a trustee, and a fund manager are all agents who act with your money or your body out of your sight. The law answers with a duty of loyalty: act only in the principal's interest, disclose conflicts, and pay when you breach. Nobody argues that doctors should be banned because they might be disloyal. The argument is over enforcing loyalty. Applied to software, the thesis is close to Lanier's own position. His economics are market economics. Data dignity is a market in which people are the sellers. His objection, stated since "Agents of Alienation" in 1995, is to agents that replace the person's judgment and serve whoever built them. An agent that is yours alone, that you can see and overrule, is not that.

**Where 2026 fails the condition.** Today's agents are built, hosted, and updated by the platforms. The shopping agent in a chatbot is paid by the merchant side; checkout partnerships were wired into the biggest assistant in September 2025. The browsing agent runs on the vendor's servers under the vendor's instructions, which you cannot read. Your memory lives in their database and does not move when you leave. That is not an agent acting for only you. It is a Siren Server's hand inside your house, and the "self-interest" it pursues is the host's. The free-market principle presumes you own your agent the way you own your labor. In 2026 you rent it.

**Where self-correction fails even with loyal agents.** Markets correct when actors bear their own costs and can exit. Markets of algorithms sometimes do neither. The 2010 flash crash was loyal trading agents, each acting for its own firm, feeding on each other until circuit breakers stopped them. Pricing algorithms in experiments learn to collude without being told to. And a market in which a million agents negotiate while a million people watch television has corrected itself only if the people are still the ones whose interests are being served. Lanier's 2000 warning applies: a system can "self-correct" toward the agents' convenience while the humans bend to fit it.

**The four conditions for the thesis to hold.**

1. Ownership: the agent runs where you can inspect it, or under a loyalty duty you can enforce, and its memory is yours and portable.
2. Liability: when it acts for you and is wrong, the vendor pays if it was disloyal, you pay if you overrode it. Today nobody pays.
3. Visibility: you remain the principal who can see and stop the action. An agent that cannot be interrupted is not an agent, it is a delegation of your personhood.
4. Circuit breakers: agent-to-agent interactions, in markets, in bidding, in negotiation, get the same stops that financial markets got after 2010.

**The motion.** "This house would make AI agents fiduciaries of their users, with liability on the vendor for disloyalty." The opposition's best line: fiduciary duties are expensive and will slow agents down. The proposition's answer: that is the point. The free market self-corrects only among actors who are loyal to someone. Make the loyalty real and the market can do its work.

### 6. What to count

**The dilemma.** Every machine number points up and every human number points down or sideways. Benchmarks are saturated and replaced by harder ones within months. Compute doubles every five months. Users approach a billion. Meanwhile 12th grade reading is at a record low, teen sadness is above its 2013 level, and the top 1 percent holds a third of the wealth. Both sets of numbers are real. The dilemma is which set gets to be called progress.

**Why this is the Lanier dilemma.** His 2000 essay said the Turing test can be passed by lowering what counts as human. Counting is how a culture lowers the bar without noticing. If the only published scoreboard is the machine's, the public learns to judge the era by the machine's gains and to treat the human losses as weather. Goodhart's law finishes the job: a measure that becomes a target stops measuring. Models are now trained toward the benchmarks that are meant to evaluate them, which is why each one saturates and a harder one is built.

**Why this is also the free-market dilemma.** Markets self-correct on what they price. What is not priced is not counted, and what is not counted is over-consumed: children's attention, contributors' data, the reader's time. The self-correction the agent thesis relies on works only if these are on the ledger. Counting is the precondition for the correction. A market that counts tokens generated and not reading scores will optimize tokens, which is what it is doing.

**A paired scorecard.** The fix is not to stop counting machines. It is to publish each machine metric next to its human twin, so the gap is visible.

| Machine metric | Human twin |
|---|---|
| Model benchmark score | Student benchmark score (NAEP, grades 4, 8, 12) |
| Weekly active users | Hours of unassisted reading, writing, and problem solving per person |
| Capital expenditure | Median real income, and the share of the gain reaching the bottom half |
| Agents deployed | Decisions still made by a visible person, with the right to interrupt |
| Tokens trained on | Share of training data with a known source, and dollars paid to the people in it |
| Time saved by AI | Time recovered for people, not reabsorbed by the feed |

The last row is Lanier's own test, which he has used since 2000: does the software make the person more capable, or does it make the person less capable so the software looks smart? That question has a number. Measure what people can do without the machine, year over year, and publish it beside what the machine can do. If both rise, it is progress. If one rises while the other falls, it is substitution, and substitution is what the 2024 report card looks like.

**The motion.** "This house would require every published AI capability benchmark to carry a human-outcome benchmark beside it." The opposition's best line: human outcomes have a thousand causes and the pairing is unfair to the machine. The proposition's answer: the pairing is not a verdict on the machine. It is the ledger the market needs in order to self-correct.
