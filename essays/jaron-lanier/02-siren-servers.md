# Siren Servers

Homer's Sirens never chased anyone. They sang, and sailors steered toward the song until the ship broke on the rocks. In Who Owns the Future? (2013) Jaron Lanier borrowed the image for the defining business of the internet age: a computer that sings "free" and collects everything that sails in.

## The definition

A Siren Server is the most powerful computer on a network, run by a party that uses it to gather data from everyone else without paying for it. The data is analyzed, the analysis produces an advantage, and the advantage is kept. The people who supplied the raw material get a service, a convenience, or nothing. They do not get a share.

That is the whole mechanism. The rest is consequences.

## Four properties

**1. It pays nothing for its inputs.** Search queries, photos, routes driven, purchases, friendships, translations, reviews. Each is worth almost nothing alone and almost everything in aggregate. The Siren Server collects the aggregate and books the input cost at zero.

**2. It keeps the analysis and exports the risk.** The owners use what they learn to make decisions that benefit themselves, and they arrange matters so that when the decisions go wrong, someone else absorbs the loss. The pattern runs from the Wall Street firms that modeled mortgage risk and sold it onward before 2008, to insurers who use data to avoid the sick, to ride platforms where the drivers own the cars and carry the accidents.

**3. It tends toward monopoly.** Information advantage compounds. The server that knows the most attracts the most users, which teaches it more, which widens its lead. Lanier argued that each niche on a network tends to end up with one Siren Server, not because its owners are uniquely clever, but because the topology rewards whoever gets ahead first.

**4. It shrinks the economy it sits on.** This claim separates Lanier from ordinary critics of Big Tech. As more human activity turns into information, and the information is treated as free, the paid economy around it contracts. Musicians, translators, journalists, and photographers, then drivers and clerks, watch their work become an uncredited input to someone else's machine. The server grows. The world it feeds on gets poorer and calls it progress.

## Not villains, architecture

Lanier was careful about blame. He did not call Google or Facebook or Amazon evil, and he did not exempt the companies he liked. He described a shape the network produces on its own, and warned that anyone who ends up running the biggest computer will become a Siren Server whether they meant to or not. Even a site built on the slogan that information wants to be free can become one. The question is never whether the owner is nice. It is who holds the data, who holds the risk, and who gets paid.

## The tell

The easiest way to spot a Siren Server is to listen for the claim that the value comes from the algorithm. It does not. The algorithm is a way of sorting what people made. Strip the people out and the machine is a very fast way of doing nothing. Lanier's remedy follows from the diagnosis: pay the people. He calls it data dignity. How to build that remedy is a separate argument. The diagnosis stands on its own.
