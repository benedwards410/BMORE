# The Man Who Sold the First Virtual Reality

**Jaron Lanier. Not Jared.** The name gets mangled so often that correcting it is a fair place to start, because the man himself has spent forty years being hard to file.

## A kid in a dome

Lanier was born in New York in 1960 and raised in Mesilla, New Mexico, after his mother died when he was nine. He and his father lived in a geodesic dome that the boy helped design. At thirteen he talked New Mexico State University into letting him take classes. He never finished a degree. He did finish a lot of music.

## A glove and a game

By the early 1980s he was in Silicon Valley writing video games. One of them, Moondust for the Commodore 64, sold well enough to fund what he really wanted to build. At Atari he met Thomas Zimmerman, an engineer who had invented a glove with optical sensors that read the bend of each finger. Lanier wanted to program computers with gestures and pictures instead of text. The glove was the missing input device.

## VPL Research

In 1984 the two founded VPL Research. The initials stood for Visual Programming Language, which tells you how far the original goal sat from the one the company became famous for. VPL built and sold, as products, things that had existed only in laboratories: the DataGlove, the EyePhone head-mounted display, the DataSuit for the whole body, and RB2, "Reality Built for Two," the first commercial system that let two people share a virtual world over a network.

NASA's Ames Research Center bought DataGloves for its simulation work. Mattel licensed the glove's design, and in 1989 a cheap plastic descendant reached millions of living rooms as the Nintendo Power Glove, which barely worked and is now a cult object. Lanier's own rigs cost tens of thousands of dollars per headset. A full two-person RB2 installation cost more than a house.

## The word

VPL needed a name for the whole field. Lanier chose "virtual reality." The phrase had appeared before in other senses, but he is the person who attached it to headsets, gloves, and shared simulated space, and who then spent the late 1980s on magazine covers explaining it. The long dreadlocks, the room full of rare instruments, and the habit of describing VR as a way to share dreams rather than shoot aliens made him the field's first public face.

## The crash

The company could not survive its own ambition. The hardware was expensive, the market was tiny, and the cash ran out. VPL went into bankruptcy in 1990. Its patents passed to a creditor and were bought by Sun Microsystems in 1999. Lanier walked away with the vocabulary, the reputation, and none of the company.

## Why it still matters

Every headset shipped since, from the Oculus Rift to the Apple Vision Pro, descends from the VPL product line: a head-mounted stereo display, tracked hands, and a shared space for more than one person. Lanier's lasting contribution was not any single device. It was insisting, before the hardware could support it, that the point of VR was to put people together, not to replace them. He told the whole story in Dawn of the New Everything (2017). He works today at Microsoft Research, and he still will not shoot the aliens.
