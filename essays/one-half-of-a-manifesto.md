# One-Half of a Manifesto (2000): Why Early False Alarms Matter

Oct 23, 2026 · @Nonya

> **Context: *Who Owns the Future?* (2013)**
>
> *Who Owns the Future?* argues that digital networks, as built today, pour wealth toward whoever runs the biggest computers while hollowing out the middle class. Simon & Schuster published it in 2013; it runs 396 pages.
>
> Lanier calls those dominant computers Siren Servers. They offer free services, collect data from everyone who uses them, and keep the value that data creates. His core line is that digital information is "really just people in disguise."
>
> The book is partly a confession. Lanier told PBS in 2013 that he helped create the open-culture rhetoric he now opposes, and failed to foresee that whoever had the biggest computer would dominate everyone else.
>
> His fix has four parts:
>
> - **Provenance:** every piece of data keeps a record of the person it came from.
> - **Two-way links:** you can see who points at your work, unlike the web's one-way links.
> - **Nanopayments:** tiny payments flow to people whenever their data adds value downstream.
> - **First-class identity:** everyone holds a commercial identity in a public system, not one owned by a company.

## The alarm

By late September 2000, Jaron Lanier had posted a 9,000-word essay on Edge.org called "One Half a Manifesto." Wired ran a version, "One-Half of a Manifesto," in its December 2000 issue.

The timing was odd. The stock market had begun to punish dot-com dreams, but faith in computing itself was untouched. Lanier, a virtual reality pioneer who described himself as inside the digital revolution but outside its dogma, used the moment to warn his own colleagues that their favorite ideas could harden into a dangerous creed.

The essay was only half a manifesto by design. Lanier wrote that a full one would also celebrate the global computer culture he loved; this half was the warning.

Many readers filed it as an overreaction. This essay argues it was something more useful: an early false alarm, wrong in several details but early enough to matter.

That distinction is the point. A warning is not only a prediction to be graded later. It is a way of telling people what to watch, while there is still time to watch it.

## What he warned

Lanier's target was a set of linked beliefs he called cybernetic totalism. He listed six:

1. Patterns of information are the best way to understand reality.
2. People are nothing more than such patterns.
3. Subjective experience either does not exist or does not matter.
4. Darwin's evolution is the one true account of all creativity and culture.
5. Moore's Law will speed up the quality of computing, not just its quantity.
6. Biology and physics will merge with computer science, and computers will soon overwhelm people, perhaps around 2020.

What he objected to most was the totalism itself. He compared it to Marxism, whose worst crime, he wrote, was claiming to be the one complete path to understanding life. A theory that explains everything leaves little room for the people it describes.

His fear was not killer robots. It was that these beliefs could "catch on in a big way" and end up built into the software that runs everyday life, where they could cause suffering for millions.

He gave a concrete example. Americans already shaped their finances to please simple credit-scoring programs, borrowing money they did not need just to feed the software the data it rewarded. People were making themselves dumber, he argued, so the software would seem smart.

His deepest worry was about money. Networks let fortunes form in months instead of decades. Add biotechnology, he asked, and might the ultra-rich and everyone else stop being recognizably the same species by mid-century?

## Why it sounded false

In 2000 the warning seemed to aim at the wrong target. That April, Sun Microsystems co-founder Bill Joy had warned in Wired that self-replicating machines could endanger humanity. Lanier's twist was stranger: computers were too clumsy to take over, and the danger lay in what people believed about them.

Salon framed the difference neatly: Joy condemned science for what it could do, Lanier for failing to see what it could not.

Edge gathered responses from a who's who of science and technology, including Freeman Dyson, Rodney Brooks, Kevin Kelly, Stewart Brand and Daniel Dennett. Brooks urged Lanier to drop the idea that people differ from machines in some ineffable way.

George Dyson answered that glitchy code was the same primordial soup that had served biology well. Kevin Kelly argued that almost anything yields new insight when imagined as code. Most critics haggled over these engineering points and passed over his larger objection to totalism.

Ray Kurzweil, a main target, replied in July 2001 that the essay mischaracterized his views and leaned on anecdotes and engineering frustrations. He also corrected the dates: he expected brain-level hardware by 2020, software around 2030, and machine dominance after that.

Harvey Blume, reviewing the essay in The American Prospect in December 2001, judged the darkest warnings out of proportion. Comparing a philosophy of computing to history's worst ideologies looked like an overreaction to a debate about software. By the standards of the time, it read as a false alarm.

## What he got wrong

Some of the alarm's specifics have aged badly. In October 2000 Lanier told Salon that software was too brittle to support thinking machines, and that code would never keep up with processing power.

In October 2024, Google's chief executive said more than a quarter of the company's new code was generated by AI, then reviewed by engineers. In March 2025, UC San Diego researchers reported that GPT-4.5, prompted to play a humanlike persona, was judged human 73% of the time in a standard three-party Turing test, more often than the real people it was paired against.

Kurzweil had argued in 2001 that the annual contest Lanier cited was a narrow version of the test, not the open conversation Turing described. The 2025 study used the stricter three-party format.

He also called artificial intelligence better understood as a belief system than a technology. That line is hard to defend in 2026. Nor did his half-joking forecast come true that buggy software would keep nearly everyone employed maintaining it. On the narrow question of whether machines would stay dumb, his critics won.

## What held up

The core of the alarm was never the timeline. It was the claim that beliefs about computers would be written into the systems people live inside, and that people would bend themselves to fit. That part reads differently in 2026.

| Lanier's 2000 warning | What followed | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A machine takeover around 2020 is fantasy | None arrived by 2020, but in September 2024 OpenAI's chief executive wrote that superintelligence may come within a few thousand days | Timeline held; the belief went mainstream |
| Machines can pass Turing tests because people lower the bar | GPT-4.5 passed in 2025 with a persona told to type casually, make typos and seem to know little; without it, 36% judged it human | Held up |
| Code will never keep up with hardware | More than a quarter of Google's new code was AI-generated by October 2024 | Missed |
| The ultra-rich could buy biology the rest cannot | Altos Labs launched in January 2022 with $3 billion to research reversing aging, reportedly backed by Jeff Bezos and Yuri Milner | Early signs, not proven |

The Turing result is the sharpest. The model passed by acting less capable than it is, which is close to Lanier's point: the test can be won by lowering what counts as human.

## The stress test: 2020 to 2026

Then came Covid. Shutdown. Lockdown. Twenty-four-hour life, transmitted through data.

Lanier's 2000 example was small: people borrowing money they did not need so a credit-scoring program would like them. The pandemic ran the same experiment at national scale. For a year or more, school, work, meals, meetings and friendship moved into software, and the software belonged to a few companies with the biggest computers. No one designed it. It was an emergency. That is exactly how Lanier said beliefs get built in: not by decree, but by whatever architecture is standing when the emergency ends.

Two things stand out. The costs landed on the people with the least slack. The class of 2024 were 8th graders when the schools closed; in math, their lowest performers fell 5 points while the 90th percentile did not move at all. The oldest adults lost the most to fraud. The bottom decile's income did not move. And the first organized backlash was physical: states pulling phones out of classrooms. People bent to fit the software, and then, slowly, bent back.

### The rise of AI, 2024 and onward

Lanier's 2000 essay said software was too brittle to keep up with hardware. By 2024 the software had caught up and the hardware had become the bottleneck.

Is modern civilization in a growth spurt never seen before? The honest answer splits. In compute, money and adoption speed, yes, faster than the personal computer or the internet. In the measures Lanier cared about, middle-class income and the share of wealth held by the bottom half, no. The exponential is in the inputs. The outputs, so far, are ordinary. That gap is the whole debate: either the outputs follow, or the inputs are a bubble, or the outputs arrive and go, as before, to whoever owns the biggest computer.

### Centralization, and the other model

In 2013 Lanier named the pattern: the Siren Server. In 2026 the map has two sides.

| | Siren Server model | Hugging Face model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who | OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and since April 2026 Meta, whose Muse Spark was its first frontier model released without open weights. Llama 4 Behemoth never shipped. | Hugging Face hosts more than 3 million public models (August 2026): DeepSeek R1 (January 2025, MIT license), Qwen, Mistral, Gemma, and even OpenAI's own gpt-oss (August 2025, Apache 2.0). |
| Where it runs | Their data center | Anywhere: a rented GPU, a laptop, a phone |
| Who holds what you type into it | Them | You |
| Who is paid for the training data | Mostly no one, so far | Mostly no one, so far |
| How you leave | You cannot take the model with you | Copy the weights |

The last two rows matter. Open weights fix where the model runs and who holds your prompts. They do nothing for the people whose work trained the model. That is Lanier's piece of the puzzle, and in 2025 it got its first price. Anthropic agreed to pay authors $1.5 billion for about 482,000 books it had taken from pirate libraries to train its models, roughly $3,000 a book. A judge gave the deal preliminary approval in September 2025 and his successor finalized it in July 2026. It is the first large-scale price put on training data: a nanopayment delivered by a courtroom instead of a protocol.

### Is a private, local model the way out?

The question on the table: should each citizen have a private, local model, trained and controlled separately, the way Lanier's data autonomy seems to imply?

Half yes.

- **What is already real.** Inference is local now. Apple ships a roughly 3-billion-parameter model that runs entirely on the phone. Open-weight models of 7 to 70 billion parameters run on a laptop once compressed. A model that lives on your device, reads your files and sends nothing out is a product, not a dream.
- **What is not.** Training. Frontier runs grow 4 to 5 times a year in compute, and the largest are projected to draw 4 to 16 gigawatts each by 2030. No citizen trains one. The realistic shape is a base model built by someone with a big computer, running on your device, tuned on your data.
- **What Lanier actually asks for.** Not a local model. His remedy, from the 2018 blueprint with Glen Weyl through the 2023 essay "There Is No A.I." to his 2026 talks, is data dignity: provenance and payment, with "mediators of individual data" bargaining for groups of people the way unions bargain for workers. A local model gives you control of what you say to it. It pays nothing to the people it was made from.

So the local model is one half of the remedy. Open weights plus local inference answer the question of who controls the computer. Provenance plus payment answer the question of who the people inside it are. Only the two together answer Lanier.

### Is there a fork in the road?

Yes, and it is visible in the numbers. On one side, 3 million open models and a $3,000-a-book precedent. On the other, more than $700 billion a year of concrete, a $5 trillion chip company, and Meta crossing from open to closed. Lanier's own essay explained how forks close: legacy code piles up, and early design choices harden into constraints no one can undo. The data centers and power plants planned for 2030 are the legacy code of this decade, and they are being poured now.

### The verdict

**Exciting.** Here is the bookkeeping on the other words.

| Word | Verdict | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Euphoric | No | The gains are not shared. The bottom decile's income did not move in 2025, and the bottom half of households holds about 2.3% of wealth. |
| Dystopian | No | Dystopia needs lock-in. Open weights exist, local inference works, a court priced training data, and 42 states pulled phones out of classrooms. The fork has not closed. |
| Mediocre | No | The capability jump is real. A machine passed the three-party Turing test, and a quarter of Google's code is machine-written. |
| Disappointing | Half | Stewardship, not technology. The costs of 2020 to 2022 fell on the people with the least slack, and almost no one has been paid for the data. |
| Exciting | Yes | The fork is open, and for the first time since 2000 the tools to take the other path exist. It is also the riskiest word here: it is the mood of the believers Lanier asked to learn humility. That is why the bookkeeping matters. |

### Next debate dilemmas

1. **Who gets paid when a model learns from your work?** The people it came from. The open question is whether that happens by protocol, as in Lanier's nanopayments, or by settlement, one lawsuit at a time.
2. **Should frontier weights be open?** Open by default for anything that runs daily life, since lock-in is the risk Lanier named. Closed only where a specific hazard is shown, and argued in public, the way Kurzweil had to argue his timeline.
3. **Who owns the compute and the power?** If the 2030 grid build-out is paid for by ratepayers and owned by four companies, the Siren Server gets a utility's moat. The test: can a university or a city rent a frontier training run at cost?
4. **Should school route through screens again?** The 2024 NAEP results and 42 state phone laws answer no by default. Screens as a tool in a classroom, not as the classroom.
5. **Does the Turing test now measure the machine or the bar?** The bar. Judge machines, and people, on what they do, not on how well they resemble each other.
6. **Can a person opt out of being data?** Not yet. The first-class identity Lanier sketched in 2013 does not exist. Building it is the next fight, and the one a local model alone cannot win.

## The case for early false alarms

Lanier's essay shows why an alarm should not be judged only by its misses. Six reasons stand out.

**Warnings work best before lock-in.** Lanier's own essay explained why: software piles up legacy code, and early design choices harden into constraints no one can easily undo. A warning that arrives after the architecture sets is a eulogy. His came in 2000, while the habits of the next decades were still forming.

**The specifics can fail while the frame survives.** Blume saw this in 2001. He doubted the darkest predictions but argued the essay's value ran beyond any one forecast: no one can list every danger of a totalizing idea in advance, but people can stay alert to it. Lanier's frame, that beliefs about computers shape how computers treat people, has outlived his forecast about code.

**Early alarms force the other side to show its work.** Kurzweil answered with a detailed plan for reaching machine intelligence and a public timeline. Brooks, Dyson and Kelly put their premises on the record. A debate held in 2000 left later readers a map of what each side believed and why.

**The costs are lopsided.** A false alarm costs some embarrassment and a few skeptical reviews. A missed alarm about technology that becomes infrastructure costs far more, for far longer.

**Insider alarms carry information outsiders cannot supply.** Lanier was not a critic looking in. He had built the gear, knew the people and, as he told Salon, had shared their faith in his twenties. When someone that close to a field breaks ranks, the dissent itself shows what the field takes for granted.

**A good alarm carries a remedy.** Lanier paired his warning with a demand that software serve the person using it, backed by something like a Hippocratic oath for programmers. Norms like that are cheap to adopt early and costly to retrofit later.

The fair objection is crying wolf: warnings that keep failing teach people to stop listening. The answer is not silence but bookkeeping. Separate the frame from the forecasts, grade each claim as it comes due, and keep the warnings that hold.

## Closing

Lanier ended his essay hoping the believers would learn humility before their day in the sun arrived. A quarter century later, that day looks closer than it did. OpenAI's chief executive writes openly about superintelligence within years, and the systems Lanier called too brittle now write code and pass for human. The fork he described has not closed, which is what makes 2026 exciting rather than settled.

He was wrong about how fast the machines would improve. He was right that what people believe about machines would be built into them, and into us. That is the value of an early false alarm: it gets the timing wrong and the stakes right, while there is still time to act.
